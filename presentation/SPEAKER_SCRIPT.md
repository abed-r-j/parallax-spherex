## Slide 1: Title (Abed, 27 words)

Hello, we are ZENITH LABS. I am Abed El Rahmane Jaafir, and this is Mohamad Alayli. Our project is ParallaX, for the challenge Planet X and SPHEREx.

## Slide 2: The challenge (Mohamad, 50 words)

Pluto, the original Planet X, was found by blinking two photographs of the same sky. Today the SPHEREx telescope images the whole sky in the infrared, again and again. The challenge asks for a public tool that shows how that sky changes, so anyone can spot what moves or varies.

## Slide 3: The problem (Abed, 43 words)

Here is the catch. SPHEREx never photographs the same spot in the same colour twice. On the left, each dot is one exposure, each at another wavelength. So when you subtract two dates, stars light up as false alarms, as on the right.

## Slide 4: The solution (Abed, 52 words)

Our answer is a chromatic template. For every frame, we predict what an unchanging sky would look like at that frame’s own wavelengths, from the spectrum each pixel showed in the other survey passes. Then we subtract. And anyone can type coordinates and watch that sky in the browser, with no server.

## Slide 5: Proof (Mohamad, 51 words)

We measured it on real frames, against two baselines. On blank sky, at the same false-alarm rate, the template finds moving sources 1.2 to 1.9 times fainter than a wavelength-matched pair. We also measured the five challenge targets: for Barnard’s Star we get 10.2 arcseconds per year, and Gaia says 10.4.

## Slide 6: Data, tools and how it was built (Mohamad, 46 words)

Everything runs on open data: SPHEREx images from the NASA/IPAC archive, plus Gaia, CatWISE, JPL Horizons and SkyBoT. We state our limits: this is not a Planet Nine detector, and bright stars saturate. We used an SI coding assistant, and we directed and checked its work.

## Slide 7: Impact and what is next (Abed, 29 words)

ParallaX lets anyone, from students to researchers, blink the infrared sky and check what they see. The code is open source and every number can be reproduced. Thank you.

## Notes for the speakers

* Every number spoken is from `docs/RESULTS.md`: 1.2 to 1.9 times fainter than the gated pair on blank sky at a matched false-detection rate (section 2.1; next to a star it is 1.1 to 1.6); Barnard's Star 10.21 +- 0.26 arcsec/yr against 10.36 from Gaia DR3 (section 3), spoken as "10.2" and "10.4".
* Do not say that ParallaX found Planet X or that it measures parallax. If asked: a real Planet Nine would be far too faint for single SPHEREx frames, and parallax is not reliably measured.
* If asked about the name: parallax is the apparent shift of a nearby object when the observer moves, which the challenge text describes; the X is from Planet X. We do not measure parallax reliably.
* If asked whose idea it was: the team chose the goal and the direction; the specific method was worked out with the SI assistant (see `docs/SI_DISCLOSURE.md`).
* If asked what "wavelength-matched pair" means: subtracting only two exposures whose colours agree within half a filter bandwidth.
* Timeline, if asked: development began on 12-13 November 2026, before the on-site event, after the local organizers allowed early preparation.

## 30-second "elevator" version (69 words, one speaker)

Pluto was found by blinking two photographs. SPHEREx photographs the whole infrared sky, but never the same spot in the same colour twice, so a simple blink fills with false alarms. ParallaX predicts each frame at its own colours and subtracts it, so far fewer false alarms are left. Type any coordinates and watch, in your browser. It is built on NASA open data, and it is open source.
